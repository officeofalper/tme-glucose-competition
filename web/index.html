Try these experiments

  1. Find the escape size. With no drugs, try tumor radius 2, 3 and 4. Small tumors are cleared; from about radius 4 the tumor drains the nutrients around it, the T cells turn grey and the tumor escapes.
  2. One drug or two? On "Large tumor", find the lowest glucose-blocking dose that works on its own. Then reset and try 50% glucose block plus 50% glutamine block together.
  3. Watch the switch back. Load "Glucose + glutamine drugs" and follow the T-cell graph: some cells go memory-like, then return to effector once the tumor shrinks and nutrients come back.
  4. Space matters. Move a large tumor close to the vessel (distance 12). Does more food help the tumor or the T cells more?
  5. Lactate. Switch lactate suppression off on "Large tumor + glucose drug that also hits T cells". How much of the T-cell failure was due to lactate?

How the model works

The tissue is a 60 × 40 grid. Glucose, glutamine and lactate diffuse between squares. The vessel on the left keeps glucose and glutamine at blood levels and washes lactate away, so squares far from the vessel get less food.

  • Tumor cells take up glucose and glutamine with high affinity, turn much of the glucose into lactate, and grow when both nutrients are available.
  • T cells are individual agents. Their fitness is glucose × glutamine supply, reduced by lactate. Effectors move toward the tumor and kill in proportion to fitness.
  • Switch: an effector whose fitness stays below 0.15 for 5 time units becomes memory-like: slow, using 20% of the fuel, not killing. Above 0.35 for 5 time units it switches back.

All values are arbitrary units chosen to show behaviour. The same model is in tme_spatial.py; the random paths of T cells differ between runs, so numbers vary a little.

Where this comes from

Based on my published review "Immunometabolism: How Metabolic Reprogramming Controls Immune Cell Activation and Function" (#93). It describes effector T cells relying on aerobic glycolysis and glutaminolysis, memory T cells relying on FAO and OXPHOS with AMPK favouring that state (Sections 2.3, 6.1), and tumor cells competing for glucose and amino acids, building up lactate and suppressing effector T-cell glycolysis (Section 6.4).

The tumor's glucose-to-lactate metabolism stands in for the Warburg effect, and its glutamine use for glutaminolysis, both discussed in my paper "Metabolic Reprogramming in Cancer Cells" (#94).

The equations, numbers, switching rule, spatial layout and drug experiments are my own modelling choices, not results from the papers. The model leaves out hypoxia, T-cell proliferation and death, new T cells arriving from blood, and real chemokine signals.
