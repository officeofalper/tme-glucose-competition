Try these experiments

  1. Find the escape point. With no drug, raise the starting tumor size from 10 to 30 one step at a time. Above roughly 20, the tumor uses up enough glucose to switch the T cells off, and it escapes.
  2. Find the weakest drug that works. Load "Large tumor" and slowly raise the tumor-blocking drug. What is the lowest dose that lets the T cells clear the tumor?
  3. Test selectivity. Load "Large tumor + selective drug", then raise the side effect on T cells. At what point does the treatment stop working, and why?

How the model works

Three quantities change over time: glucose G, tumor cells N, and T-cell activity E (0 to 1). The number of T cells T is fixed. Each cell type's glucose use saturates as G/(K + G); tumor cells have the lower K, so they keep feeding when glucose is scarce.

dG/dt = supply − 0.1·G
        − 0.05·f_tumor(G)·N
        − 0.02·f_T(G)·T
dN/dt = 0.3·f_tumor(G)·N·(1 − N/200)
        − 0.04·E·T·N
E     = f_T(G)

A drug multiplies f by (1 − block). The animation shows the same numbers: each glucose dot is about 1/8 of a unit, each purple cell is one tumor cell unit. All values are arbitrary units chosen to show behaviour.

Where this comes from

Based on my published review "Immunometabolism: How Metabolic Reprogramming Controls Immune Cell Activation and Function" (#93). It describes how effector T cells rely on aerobic glycolysis, and how tumor cells in the tumor microenvironment compete for glucose and suppress effector T-cell glycolysis (Section 6.4).

The tumor's stronger glucose uptake is a simple stand-in for the Warburg effect discussed in my paper "Metabolic Reprogramming in Cancer Cells" (#94).

The equations, numbers, escape threshold and drug experiments are my own modelling choices, not results from the papers. This is a teaching model: it leaves out lactate, hypoxia, amino acids, T-cell proliferation and space.
